Demand forecast dashboard

The buying team commits inventory a quarter ahead, and right now that decision is made by squinting at last year's numbers in a pivot table. Build a forecast the merchandising director can read, question, and act on in one sitting.

Difficulty · Intermediate ~90–120 minutes Uses: Modules 1, 4

1. The business decision

You're the demand-planning analyst at Harrow & Vale, a direct-to-consumer home-goods brand with about a dozen products across four categories: bedding, kitchen, bath, and add-ons. The company manufactures overseas, so purchase orders have to be placed roughly a quarter before the inventory is needed. Once a PO is signed, the money is committed.

Today the merchandising director sets those quantities by opening last year's sales in a pivot table and adjusting by feel. Last Q4 that approach failed twice in the same buy. The bath numbers looked like a category in decline, so the company cut its order — and bath then sold out in the middle of the holiday season. The bedding numbers looked steady, so the company ordered roughly what it had ordered the year before, and spent the following spring discounting what didn't sell. Both mistakes are visible in the files you're about to open. Neither is obvious from a simple category pivot table.

The Q4 buy is due in a week. You have sales history through the end of September 2025. Your job is to produce a 13-week unit forecast for the quarter beginning 6 October 2025, and to hand the director something they can actually use in the buying meeting — not a spreadsheet tab they'll never open, but a dashboard that shows what you're projecting, how the projection compares with last year, and where the forecast is most uncertain.

You don't need a sophisticated model. You need a defensible one, and you need to be able to explain it in a sentence.

The point of this case In some workflows, there is a known answer you can reconcile to. Forecasting is different. There is no single correct future number. Your job is to build a reasonable forecast, test whether the method has earned your trust, and make the assumptions visible to the decision-maker.

2. Data available

Three files sit in a folder on your laptop. Sample versions of all three are linked below so you can follow along:

  • sales_history.csv — weekly units sold by SKU from January 2023 through the end of September 2025. Each row also carries the average unit price actually realized that week, whether a promotion was running, and how many days that week the SKU was out of stock.
  • product_catalog.csv — one row per SKU: its name, its category, when it launched, whether it's still active or has been discontinued, its unit cost, and its current list price. This file describes the catalog as it stands today, not necessarily as it stood earlier in the history.
  • marketing_calendar.csv — every campaign the brand ran over the same historical period, one row per week per channel, with the spend behind it. Marketing is an important driver of some of the spikes in the sales history.

The Q4 2025 marketing plan has not yet been finalized. You therefore know how marketing affected historical demand, but you do not know exactly what campaigns will run during the quarter you are forecasting. That is a real information gap, not something you should quietly fill in. Decide how your forecast will deal with it and state the assumption clearly.

The complication is that this history records what the company sold, which is not always the same as what customers wanted. Some observations may also be less comparable than they first appear. Treat these files as evidence to investigate, not as a clean forecasting table that is ready to model.

3. Explore before you forecast

Start by exploring the three files together. Do not build the dashboard yet. Your first job is to understand what the data actually represent and what could make a historical number misleading.

Use your agent to inspect the files, but don't outsource the judgment. Ask questions such as:

  • What does one row mean in each file?
  • How do the files relate to one another?
  • Which series have enough history to support a forecast?
  • Are there weeks you should not take at face value?
  • Has the catalog, labeling, or business context changed over time?
  • What information would you want before treating a historical spike or decline as evidence of future demand?

Keep short notes on anything you discover that could materially affect the forecast. Expect this step to change what you plan to build.

Don't skip exploration An agent can produce a polished forecast from almost any column of numbers. That does not mean the column deserves to be forecast. Understanding what the business was doing during those weeks is part of the analysis.

4. Plan your approach

Before execution, ask your agent to propose a plan and review it. Your plan should answer the questions below. You do not need to use a sophisticated forecasting model; a simple method that you understand and can test is often preferable.

  • What are you forecasting? Your final answer must be at the category level, but you may forecast categories directly or forecast individual SKUs and aggregate them. Choose deliberately and explain why.
  • What counts as usable history? Decide how you will treat observations that may not reflect unconstrained demand, as well as products with unusual or incomplete histories.
  • How will you handle marketing? Historical promotions and campaign spending may explain past spikes, but the future campaign plan is unknown. Decide whether your forecast represents a baseline, assumes a historical pattern repeats, uses scenarios, or takes another defensible approach.
  • What forecasting method will you use? Be able to explain the method in one or two sentences that a non-analyst could understand and challenge.
  • How will you test it? Hold out a stretch of history, forecast it as though you could not see the answer, and compare your result with what actually happened.
  • What simple benchmark will you compare against? For example, would your method beat simply using the same week or quarter last year?

Do not start coding until you can explain the logic of your proposed workflow.

5. Build the forecast and dashboard

Once you are comfortable with the plan, execute it. Let the agent handle implementation details where appropriate, but keep your attention on the assumptions, choices, and checks that determine whether the result is useful.

What the forecast has to do

  • Cover the 13 weeks beginning 6 October 2025, at the category level, in units.
  • State its method in plain language — one or two sentences a non-analyst can follow and challenge.
  • Be tested on historical data by holding out a period whose answer you already know and reporting the resulting forecast error.
  • Be compared with a simple baseline, such as the same period last year. A more complicated method is useful only if it improves the decision.
  • Explain which SKUs or observations it excludes, adjusts, or treats specially, and why.
  • Handle periods where observed sales may have been constrained rather than chosen. You are not expected to recover the exact amount of lost demand. Choose a reasonable treatment — such as excluding, replacing, or flagging those periods — and explain the assumption.
  • Identify the categories or forecasts you trust least and explain why. Formal prediction intervals are not required.

What the dashboard has to do

  • Be a single self-contained HTML file that opens in a browser by double-clicking it. No server, no install, nothing to run first. The director will open it on their own laptop.
  • Show total forecast units for the quarter and the same quarter last year, with the change between them.
  • Chart history and forecast together, by category, with the forecast visually distinct from the actuals so nobody mistakes one for the other.
  • Give a category-by-month table with the percentage change against last year.
  • Flag the forecasts you trust least where the director will see the flag next to the number, not buried in a footnote.
  • Carry a short assumptions panel: the forecasting method, the treatment of unusual observations, the marketing assumption, anything excluded or adjusted, and the limitations you would want raised if someone leaned on the forecast too hard.
Spend your time on the judgment Do not spend most of the exercise manually styling cards, charts, or buttons. Let the agent help with implementation and visual polish. Your job is to make sure the underlying analysis deserves to be presented professionally.

6. Verify the output

A forecast dashboard is unusually good at looking finished while being wrong. The charts render, the totals add up, the percentages are formatted — and the underlying number can still be a guess built on contaminated history. Now inspect your work against the source files and the checks below.

Do not treat this checklist as a list of patches to make blindly. For each issue, first determine whether it actually affects your workflow and why.

Check Questions to ask
Source protection Were the three original CSVs left untouched, with everything new written to separate files?
Duplicate records Are any source rows duplicated? If so, would your workflow count them twice?
Cleaning decisions Is every material change made to the raw data documented, or did rows get dropped, deduplicated, renamed, or filled in silently?
Constrained weeks Were weeks with days out of stock treated as potentially suppressed sales rather than automatically interpreted as weak demand? What did the workflow do with them, and can you defend that choice?
Catalog changes How were discontinued products and recently launched products handled? Is either being extrapolated as though it had a normal, complete history?
Category continuity Do historical category totals refer to the same underlying products and labels across the full period? If a category name changed, were the old and new labels reconciled correctly?
Missing periods Are any expected SKU-weeks absent? If so, did the workflow distinguish between a true zero and a missing observation?
Marketing assumption Does the forecast quietly assume future campaign activity that is not in the files, or is the assumption about promotions and marketing made explicit?
Holdout accuracy Is there an actual reported error figure from a period the model could not see, or only a claim that the method is reasonable?
Baseline comparison Did the forecasting method beat a simple benchmark? If not, is the extra complexity actually helping?
Known totals Pick a category and a quarter, total it yourself from the raw CSV, and compare it with what the dashboard shows for the same period. Do they match?
Method transparency Can you state in one sentence how the forecast was produced, and would the director be able to challenge it on that basis?
Chart integrity Are the axes labeled with real units, is the forecast clearly separated from the actuals, and does every plotted point trace back to data that exists?
Stated assumptions Does the dashboard tell the reader what was excluded, adjusted, or assumed and where the forecast is weakest, or does it present one confident number with no context?
Repeatability Next quarter there will be a new export. Could you rerun this workflow quickly, or would you rebuild the analysis from scratch?

When something doesn't reconcile, find out why before asking the agent to patch the number. The problem may come from the source data, from a cleaning decision, from the forecasting method, or from the dashboard code — those are four different problems with four different fixes.

7. Improve the first version

Verification is useful only if it changes what you do next. Review the issues you found and identify the single most consequential weakness in your first version. Fix it, then regenerate the forecast and dashboard.

Before you finish, write down two short answers:

  1. What did verification uncover that you did not notice during the first build?
  2. Why does the revised version deserve more trust than the first one?

8. One possible solution

Work the case yourself before opening this. A worked solution earns its keep when you hold it against your own output, your own assumptions, and your own verification notes.

Open the sample solution dashboard → — a self-contained page of the kind section 5 asks for, built from the same three CSVs. It opens in a new tab so you can keep the verification table in section 6 beside it.

The solution reached 104,869 units for the quarter, against 86,171 reported a year earlier. That headline splits into roughly ten points of like-for-like growth and eleven points contributed by a product that did not exist in the prior year, and the dashboard reports the split rather than the single number. Its main decisions, each of which you can compare against your own:

  • Grain. The four categories were forecast directly instead of forecasting twelve SKUs and adding them up. Aggregation absorbs the SKU-level defects in this dataset, and the buying decision is made at category level anyway.
  • Method. Each forecast week is the matching week a year earlier multiplied by a growth rate measured over the trailing 52 weeks. Black Friday 2025 falls exactly 364 days after Black Friday 2024, so the peak weeks line up without adjustment — a piece of luck worth checking rather than assuming.
  • Constrained weeks. One SKU was out of stock through the entire prior-year peak, one of those weeks for all seven days. Roughly 2,800 units of demand were reconstructed, but only after confirming that the other two products in the same category rose over the same weeks. Had the whole category fallen together, the drop would have been read as genuine weakness and left alone.
  • Catalog changes. The discontinued product was removed from both years so the comparison holds the same products on each side, and the ten-week-old product was forecast on its own line with an explicit range rather than folded into a category total.
  • The information gap. The Q4 marketing plan is absent from the files, so the solution derives a calendar from the pattern in three years of history and labels it as inferred on the page itself.
  • Accuracy. Held out against a quarter the method could not see, it was 14.9% off week by week and 2.5% off on the quarter total, against 17.6% for a same-period-last-year benchmark. Both figures appear on the dashboard, and the weekly figure is the reason it recommends trusting the quarter and distrusting individual weeks.

The dashboard closes with a step-by-step method section that names the data used, the calculation performed, and the number produced at each stage. Reading that section against the verification table in section 6 is the fastest way to see which checks a solution can actually answer.

One answer among several Several of the decisions above have defensible alternatives. Forecasting SKUs and aggregating is reasonable if you handle the churn explicitly. Leaving the stockout weeks untouched is reasonable if you say plainly that the result understates demand. What separates a good submission from a weak one is whether the choice was made deliberately, stated on the page, and defended — not whether it matches this one.
