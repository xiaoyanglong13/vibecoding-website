Why the percentages repeat across months. Growth is applied as one rate per category, so the year-on-year change is the same in every month by construction — the month-to-month movement lives in the unit counts, which carry last year’s seasonal shape. Only the total row varies, because the mix between categories shifts.
What the flags mean. LOW CONFIDENCE means the number rests on something the data cannot pin down — treat it as a planning range, not a target. MEDIUM is the best this dataset supports: two prior Q4s is a thin base for any seasonal forecast. No category qualifies as high confidence.

Assumptions and limitations

What these numbers are

Unconstrained demand, not expected sales. They estimate what customers would buy if everything stayed in stock. Last year Bath did not — so these figures are deliberately higher than a straight projection of what was sold. Use them to set buy quantities. They are not a revenue forecast.

Method

Each forecast week is last year’s matching week multiplied by that category’s growth rate. Black Friday 2025 falls exactly 364 days after Black Friday 2024, so last year’s peak week lines up with this year’s without adjustment — which is not true in most years and removes a common source of error.

Growth is measured over the trailing 52 weeks. Windows of 13, 26 and 52 weeks scored the same on accuracy, so the most stable was chosen; the shorter windows imply faster growth and appear in the upper end of the shaded range.

Unusual observations and adjustments
  • Stockouts reconstructed (3,671 units). Six SKU-weeks were out of stock. BTH-300 was out through the entire Q4 2024 peak — Black Friday week and the two after, one of them all seven days — costing an estimated 2,804 units. Since the forecast multiplies last year forward, leaving that in would have projected a supply failure as if it were demand.
  • The reconstruction was gated on a control test. The other two Bath SKUs were in stock those weeks and sold more, not less, so the category was healthy and only that one product ran out. Had all three fallen together it would have been read as genuine softness and left alone.
  • 11 duplicate rows removed — every SKU is recorded twice for the week of 3 June 2024.
  • Three SKU-weeks are absent, not zero. KIT-220 has no rows for three weeks in February 2024. They are left missing rather than filled with zeros, because a zero would be read as no demand. They fall outside every window this forecast uses.
  • Two SKUs show prices above list. KIT-210 sells above its catalog list price in 59 weeks. Price is not a model input here, so this does not affect the forecast, but it is worth someone checking the catalog.
  • Category renamed mid-series. Two SKUs move from “Accessories” to “Add-ons” in January 2025; treated as one continuous category.
  • BED-120 excluded from both years. Discontinued March 2025, so including last year’s 2,378 units would show a fake decline in Bedding.
  • ACC-420 forecast separately. Launched 21 July 2025 with 11 weeks of history and a share still climbing. Modelled as a ratio to the rest of Add-ons, with the ramp damped — extrapolating its trend straight would put it above 65%% of the category by December, which is not credible.
Marketing assumption

The marketing file stops at 8 September 2025, so Q4 campaigns are inferred, not confirmed. Every campaign in three years of history falls on the first Mondays of its month, except Black Friday which tracks the holiday. Applying that rule gives:

  • 24 November — Black friday
  • 1 December — Black friday, Holiday gifting
  • 8 December — Holiday gifting
  • 15 December — Holiday gifting

Spend is assumed about 7%% above 2024, in line with every campaign this year. Somebody almost certainly has the real Q4 plan, and dropping it in would improve this forecast more than any modelling change.

Limitations — read these before leaning on the forecast
  • Only two prior Q4s exist (2023 and 2024), and they are structurally different: 2023 ran a five-week promo block, 2024 only four because Thanksgiving fell late. 2025 matches 2024, so 2024 is weighted heavily — which means the most important part of this forecast effectively rests on one observation. No modelling choice fixes that.
  • Trust the quarter, not the week. On a held-out test of Q4 2024 the method was 14.9% off week by week but 2.5% off on the quarter total. It is sound for budgets and buy quantities, not for deciding which week to land inventory.
  • Bath is partly modelled. Roughly 11%% of its Q4 2024 base is reconstructed rather than observed. The control test supports it, but if that stockout coincided with genuine softness, Bath is overstated.
  • Nothing here knows about price or discount depth. Neither is a model input, and neither is known for Q4 2025.
  • The forecast assumes the stockout does not repeat. Whether BTH-300 is in stock this November is a supply question this data cannot answer.
  • A third Q4 would help more than anything else. If 2022 data exists anywhere, it is worth more than any refinement to the method.

Method, step by step

Every number on this page traces back through these steps. Each shows the data used, the calculation, and what came out.

Step 1

Build the panel

Data in
sales_history.csv — 1,577 rows of SKU × week units
Calculation
Drop rows duplicated on (week, SKU); rename Accessories to Add-ons; add up units by category and week
Output
1,566 rows after removing 11 duplicates → 144 weeks × 4 categories
Step 2

Restrict to comparable products

Data in
the panel, plus product_catalog.csv (status, launch date)
Calculation
Hold out BED-120 (discontinued March 2025) and ACC-420 (launched July 2025) so both years contain the same products
Output
a 10-SKU like-for-like panel; the two held-out SKUs are handled separately
Step 3

Find the censored weeks

Data in
the stockout_days column
Calculation
Flag any SKU-week where stock ran out at all
Output
6 weeks of 1,566 — but three are consecutive weeks of BTH-300 in the Q4 2024 peak, which is exactly the period this forecast is built from
Step 4

Test whether the category actually fell

Data in
the other two Bath SKUs over the same three weeks
Calculation
Compare each against the same week last year. If every SKU dropped, the cause is demand and nothing is corrected
Output
BTH-310 +35%, −9%, +7% and BTH-320 +35%, −1%, +15% — siblings held up, so this was a supply failure
Step 5

Reconstruct the missing demand

Data in
BTH-300’s uncensored Q4 2024 weeks and its 2023 values at the same Black-Friday offsets
Calculation
Measure the SKU’s growth on weeks it could actually ship (×1.115), then apply it to last year’s value for each missing week
Output
1,346, 1,457, 1,356 → 1,500, 1,624, 1,511 against 997, 277, 558 observed; 2,804 units recovered for BTH-300, 3,671 across all six weeks
Step 6

Measure growth

Data in
the trailing 52 weeks to 29 Sep 2025, against the same weeks a year earlier — after the correction above
Calculation
Recent units ÷ year-ago units, per category
Output
Add-ons 1.094, Bath 1.110, Bedding 1.081, Kitchen 1.115. Windows of 13, 26 and 52 weeks tied on accuracy, so the steadiest was used
Step 7

Line up the calendar

Data in
Thanksgiving dates for 2024 and 2025
Calculation
Black Friday week 2025 minus Black Friday week 2024
Output
24 Nov 2025 − 25 Nov 2024 = 364 days exactly, so last year’s peak maps onto this year’s with no adjustment — unusual, and it removes a common error
Step 8

Forecast the existing products

Data in
corrected like-for-like units from the matching week last year
Calculation
Last year’s week × that category’s growth
Output
13 weeks × 4 categories, totalling 95,405 units
Step 9

Forecast the new SKU on its own

Data in
ACC-420’s 11 weeks since launch, as a share of the rest of Add-ons
Calculation
Its share rose from 0.139 to 0.363, about +0.022 a week. Continuing that line would exceed 0.65 by December, so the ramp is damped toward a plateau
Output
9,464 units (range 7,144 to 10,365) — kept on its own row because this is the softest number here
Step 10

Build the range

Data in
four runs: three growth windows, plus the 2023 promo shape
Calculation
Take the lowest and highest of the four for every week
Output
the shaded band on each chart — a spread of real scenarios rather than a statistical interval, which two seasons cannot support
Step 11

Test it on a year we already know

Data in
everything up to 30 Sep 2024, forecasting Q4 2024, scored against what happened
Calculation
Add up the weekly misses and divide by the actual total
Output
14.9% off week by week, 2.5% on the quarter total. On uncorrected data it was 16.9%, and Bath alone improved from 22.9% to 11.7% — evidence the stockout fix is doing real work
Step 12

Check it beats a simple benchmark

Data in
the same held-out quarter, forecast as last year’s weeks with no growth adjustment at all
Calculation
Score the benchmark the same way and compare
Output
benchmark 17.6% against the method’s 14.9% — the growth adjustment is worth 2.6 points. A method that could not beat this would not be worth its complexity
Step 13

Set the confidence flags

Data in
the per-category backtest error, plus what each forecast structurally depends on
Calculation
Rank by measured error, then adjust for SKU churn and for how much of the base is modelled rather than observed
Output
Add-ons 19.6%; Bath 11.7%; Bedding 15.9%; Kitchen 17.6% — Add-ons is flagged low because ACC-420 is 29% of it
Step 14

Reconcile

Data in
all of the above
Calculation
Check the parts add up to the whole
Output
Q4 2024 as reported 86,171 + 2,804 reconstructed = 88,975 demand, − 2,378 BED-120 = 86,597 like-for-like base. Forecast 95,405 existing + 9,464 new = 104,869
Two things to hold onto. The forecasting method was picked because it won a backtest run on a single year — one fold is not a strong basis for choosing between methods. And the stockout reconstruction in step 5 is an estimate, not an observation: those units were never sold, and the figure is what the evidence suggests would have been.
